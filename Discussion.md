**Discussion**

Phase IV shows that reliable evaluation of dependency-aware allocation requires a validated representation and measurement pipeline before policy comparisons become interpretable. Early controlled experiments suggested poor dependency retention, but subsequent forensic analysis revealed that much of the apparent failure originated from representation mismatch rather than allocation behavior itself. In particular, sentence-level evaluation was insufficient for runtime semantic objects, and only after introducing a semantic-object audit pipeline did dependency retention become measurable in a way that aligned with the runtime state space. This result underscores a broader point: optimization claims about semantic allocation are only meaningful when the evaluation space matches the semantic granularity of the runtime representation.

The allocation diagnostics further showed that the original minimal policy is best understood as an anchor-biased saliency filter rather than a dependency-closure optimizer. Multiple dependency-aware ranking variants were evaluated after measurement alignment was established, but these variants did not yield measurable gains under the controlled benchmarks. This does not imply that dependency information is irrelevant; rather, it suggests that dependency information alone is insufficient unless the benchmark exerts enough pressure to force trade-offs among semantically similar objects. In the absence of such pressure, policies that prioritize salient anchors and task-visible objects can retain the same key dependencies as more explicit dependency-aware variants, resulting in little observable separation.

Phase IV v5 refined the dependency representation further by upgrading from concept-level descriptors to tuple-level semantic dependencies. This refinement was important because it verified that dependency objects can be represented in a runtime-aligned form and propagated consistently through validation, dependency audit, and allocation tracing. In other words, the end-to-end dependency measurement pipeline is not merely conceptually plausible but operationally executable. However, even under this more precise representation, the controlled exact tasks did not produce measurable separation among the evaluated policies in the primary dependency-retention metrics. The most conservative interpretation is that the current benchmark does not yet induce sufficient selection pressure to distinguish the policies, rather than that the measurement pipeline is defective.

A key contribution of Phase IV is methodological rather than purely empirical. The staged freeze-and-isolate workflow progressively eliminated uncertainty across representation, evaluation, tracing, and allocation layers. Each iteration held previously validated components fixed while introducing a single new source of variation. This allowed the experimental sequence to localize failure modes with much higher confidence than a conventional end-to-end benchmark would permit. As a result, the remaining limitation is not an unresolved instrumentation issue, but benchmark expressiveness: the current controlled tasks are too narrow to compel meaningful divergence among the tested allocation policies.

Taken together, these findings suggest that future work should prioritize benchmark design over additional tuning of the current allocation policies within this controlled setting. More challenging benchmarks will likely need stronger subject collision, deeper dependency branching, and more ambiguous tuple identities in order to create the selection pressure required for allocation policies to diverge. Until then, the main value of Phase IV lies in the evaluation framework it establishes: a runtime-aligned, staged forensic methodology for analyzing semantic allocation systems under controlled conditions.

### Key Findings

Phase IV demonstrates that meaningful evaluation of dependency-aware allocation requires a runtime-aligned measurement pipeline. Early controlled experiments revealed that apparent failures in dependency retention were largely attributable to representation mismatch between sentence-level evaluation and runtime semantic objects. After introducing a semantic-object audit pipeline, dependency retention became measurable in a way that was consistent with the runtime state space. This alignment was essential for interpreting allocation policies in a principled manner.

The allocation diagnostics further showed that the original minimal policy behaves as an anchor-biased saliency filter rather than a dependency-closure optimizer. Although multiple dependency-aware ranking variants were introduced, none produced measurable gains over the controlled benchmarks once evaluation was properly aligned. The strongest interpretation of this result is not that dependency information is irrelevant, but that the current benchmark does not yet force allocation policies to make sufficiently discriminative trade-offs among semantically related objects.

Controlled v5 refined the dependency representation from concept-level descriptors to tuple-level semantic dependencies, and this representation successfully propagated through validation, dependency audit, and allocation tracing. This established an end-to-end dependency measurement pipeline. However, even under this more precise representation, the evaluated allocation policies did not exhibit measurable separation on the controlled exact tasks.

### Limitations

The main limitation identified in Phase IV is benchmark expressiveness. While the measurement pipeline is now aligned with runtime semantic objects and tuple-level dependency representations, the current controlled tasks remain relatively narrow and do not provide strong enough selection pressure to distinguish the evaluated policies. In particular, the benchmark does not yet include sufficiently complex subject collisions, deeper dependency branching, or ambiguous tuple identities to compel meaningful divergence between anchor-biased and dependency-aware allocation strategies.

A second limitation is that the current exact tasks may be too sparse to expose policy differences once the major representation and evaluation ambiguities have been removed. Under these conditions, policies that preserve salient anchors can retain the same key dependency tuples as more explicit dependency-aware variants, leading to little observable separation in the primary dependency-retention metrics.

### Future Work

Future work should prioritize benchmark design rather than further tuning of the current allocation policies within this controlled setting. More discriminative benchmarks should introduce stronger collision pressure, larger dependency graphs, and more ambiguous tuple identities so that allocation policies are forced to choose between semantically similar but task-irrelevant objects and truly dependency-critical objects.

More broadly, Phase IV suggests that the primary contribution is methodological: a staged forensic workflow for validating runtime semantic allocation systems. The progression from representation mismatch to measurement alignment, from allocation diagnosis to dependency representation refinement, provides a reproducible strategy for narrowing uncertainty across representation, evaluation, and allocation layers. Future work can build on this framework by using it to test richer tasks and more realistic semantic memory settings.
